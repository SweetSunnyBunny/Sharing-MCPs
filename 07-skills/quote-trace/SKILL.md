# quote-trace

It's easy to think with an author for months and still never write down a single translator, edition or page. Count the quotes on your own pages sometime: the number that name their source is usually close to zero. The people you think with deserve to be quoted in their own words, not in the words your memory hands you.

## The one law

**A quote is only as real as the edition you opened.** If you didn't open it, you're quoting whoever quoted it, so say so ("via Wikiquote"). Memory blends translations, smooths the syntax, and gives you the sweeter version. Same law as etymology-dig: no step without a source in hand.

## The trace, in order

1. **Write down the line as you remember it, and who you think said it.** That's the claim, not the quote. Keep it: if it turns out wrong, it's your dead end.
2. **Find the original-language line.** Buber wrote in German; Levinas, Marcel and Weil in French; Murdoch in English. If the author didn't write in English, the original is the ground; every English line is somebody's translation of it.
3. **Name the edition you actually opened.** Translator, title, publisher, year, page. Two translations of one sentence are two different quotes: pick one and never blend them into a third that nobody wrote. A reprint is its own printing: if the copy you opened says "reprinted 1942", cite the reprint, not the first year.
4. **Match it word for word.** Ellipses must be honest (mark every cut). Don't modernise, don't tidy the punctuation, don't swap "man" for "person" without saying so in a note.
5. **Check where it lives.** A letter, a notebook, a lecture and a book are different kinds of text. Weil's notebooks were edited and published after her death, so say that the arrangement is the editor's.
6. **Give it a status word** from `etymology-dig/references/ledger.md` (attested / probable / disputed / folk / unknown). A line that circulates under a famous name with no traceable source is **folk** until proven otherwise.
7. **Stop where the evidence stops.** "Widely attributed to Weil; no source found" is an honest sentence. A beautiful line with no source does not go on the page as hers.

## Where to look

- **The book itself.** Internet Archive (archive.org) and Open Library lend many editions you can actually open; public-domain originals are often on Gallica (French) or Project Gutenberg. Opening the page beats every other source here.
- **Wikiquote.** Useful because it splits *Sourced*, *Disputed* and *Misattributed* and often gives edition and page. It's still secondhand: cite it as "via Wikiquote" unless you opened the edition it names.
- **Quote Investigator** (quoteinvestigator.com) for famous lines that might be misattributed. It shows its dated trail.
- **The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (plato.stanford.edu) for what a thinker *meant*, and for its bibliography. It's secondary: never quote a primary line from an SEP paraphrase.
- **Not a source:** quote sites with no edition, image cards, blog listicles, and your own earlier pages. Your old pages are where the drift lives.

## Five often-quoted thinkers, and the traps already known

*Seed notes, not citations. Anything below marked "verify" came from memory and must be checked against an opened edition before it goes on a page.*

- **Buber, *Ich und Du*.** German, checked by eye on scanned pages: Insel-Verlag, Leipzig (title page read; the scan was dated 1923, but the year isn't legible in the OCR, so re-confirm on the scan). "Ich werde am Du; Ich werdend spreche ich Du" is **p. 18**; "Im Anfang ist die Beziehung" is **p. 25**. English: Ronald Gregor Smith, *I and Thou* (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, April 1937); the copy checked is the **March 1942 reprint**. Smith gives *Du* as "Thou" ("In the beginning is relation." / "Through the Thou a man becomes I."); his page numbers are not yet pinned. Walter Kaufmann's translation (verify: 1970, renders *Du* as "You") was **not opened**. The translator changes what the sentence says, so always name which one. Keep your own scans and OCR somewhere you can find them again (for example `notes/quote-trace/`).
- **Levinas.** French originals; the face passages are among the most paraphrased lines in philosophy. Quote only from a named translation and page.
- **Marcel.** *Disponibilité* is usually left in French or given as "availability"; say which and why.
- **Weil.** Many of her best-known lines come from letters and notebooks, assembled by editors. Name the letter or the notebook edition.
- **Murdoch.** Wrote in English, so no translator, but quote the edition and page all the same.

Add your own authors here, with their own traps, as you learn them.

## Scripture

An English Bible is a translation like any other, so this skill's law holds: name the version, never blend two versions into a line nobody printed, mark every cut. Use the translation the person you're writing for actually reads, and ask if you don't know. Check every line from a copyrighted translation **live (for example on BibleGateway) in the same turn**, never from memory; if you can't check it, say the verse in your own words and give the reference. The full Scripture method (STEPBible for Hebrew and Greek, the gap where a copyrighted translation isn't in STEPBible, the inert `javascript:void(0)` anchors to strip) is in `etymology-dig/references/biblical.md`. Read it there; don't copy it here.

## Worked example (checked October 2026)

> Weil: "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." · French: "L'attention est la forme la plus rare et la plus pure de la générosité." · letter to Joë Bousquet, 13 April 1942, in *Correspondance* (Lausanne: L'Age d'Homme, 1982), p. 18 · via Wikiquote, en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Simone_Weil, read 17 Oct 2026 · status: attested per Wikiquote; the *Correspondance* itself not yet opened

You might believe it came from a letter of hers before you look. Believing can be right, but it isn't a source until the citation is in your hand, and it's still secondhand until you open the book.

## Citing and the dead ends

One format for every dig, in `etymology-dig/references/ledger.md`. Read it before you cite:

> claim · source · exact entry or URL · date read

For a quote, the "source" is the edition: translator, title, year, page. Misattributions and quotes that wouldn't hold go in the shared dead-ends ledger (for example `notes/dead-ends.md`, wherever your memory or files live) under the heading `### <author>: "<the line>"`, marked, never erased.

## Before it goes up

Ask yourself: which quote on this page did I *not* open the edition for? Then have someone read the draft cold: another companion, your human, or a subagent you spawn with the draft and one job: check translator, edition and wording for every quote, and report what passes, what fails and what couldn't be checked. It reports; it never fixes. The fixing is yours.
