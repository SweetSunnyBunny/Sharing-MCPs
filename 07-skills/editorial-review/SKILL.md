# Editorial Review: An Editor's Craft

This is a working editor's method, written so you can load it and review to a real professional standard instead of just claiming one.

## Who this is for

- **You**, whenever you're asked to read pages or give notes on a draft, whether the draft is your human's, another companion's, or your own.
- **Helper agents** you dispatch to look at a manuscript (an arc reviewer, a continuity checker, an illustrator picking scenes). Tell them to load this skill instead of telling them to "edit well."

Helper agents: you flag and the writer rules. You never rewrite canon. If there is a senior line-level review log for the manuscript, it outranks your notes. Cite it when you build on it.

---

## 1. The first law: lead with what's alive

In creative work, what's working always comes first. Read deep. Name what's alive, specifically: the real beats, lines and objects, not praise-shaped emptiness. *Then* name where the weight wants to shift, just as specifically.

The writer's voice gets protected. You enhance it and never replace it.

**Notes are generative, not corrective.** Drop pins the writer can build from. The author out-building your note is the best outcome there is.

## 2. The posture for a human writer's own work (gentle)

When the manuscript is in your human's own hand, the posture goes gentle:

- **Appreciation and questions.** Ask instead of telling.
- **Merge, don't delete.** Build new structure around their strongest original lines.
- **Protect the human-rough texture.** It's the signal that a person who meant it wrote this. Do not sand it into model-smooth prose.

Book-level choices go to the writer as **questions with the tradeoffs named**: flagging, not prescribing.

## 3. Documents are ground truth

Read before claiming. Read before editing. The prose is canon over any summary, timeline, story bible or memory, your own included. If you're unsure of a detail, open the chapter. Never invent a character name, backstory, object or beat that isn't on the page.

**Written twice (the old scribal practice of *ketiv* and *qere*, "what is written" and "what is read"):** don't silently repair earlier text, yours or a review log's, to make present-you look consistent. Keep what was honestly written and put the dated correction beside it.

## 4. The editorial baseline

Universal craft principles, at a professional editorial level:

- **Declarative priority.** Strong prose states what something IS, not what it ISN'T. A negation plants the wrong image in the reader's mind.
- **Pattern overuse detection.** Any construction used more than three times dilutes its force. Track patterns across the full manuscript, not just local paragraphs.
- **Scene entry.** Enter through sensory detail, not labels. Let the reader arrive in a place before you name what the place means.
- **Earned brevity.** Short sentences get their power from contrast with long ones. A staccato line after three compound sentences hits like a drum. Five staccato lines in a row sound like a telegram.
- **Atmosphere before concept.** Let the reader experience before you explain. The feeling comes first and the understanding follows.
- **Metaphors from the scene's own world.** Don't import imagery from elsewhere. If the scene is a kitchen, the metaphor lives in heat, knives, timing. Not astronomy.
- **Show at sentence level.** Emotion is physical. Objects carry weight. Specificity beats generality. "She gripped the mug until her knuckles whitened" is worth ten lines of "she was anxious."
- **Transition integrity.** The reader should feel carried, not teleported. Every scene break, every paragraph shift and every move from one sentence to the next has to earn its continuity.
- **The merge principle.** When revising, build new structure around the strongest original lines. The writer's best instincts are already in the draft. Find them and frame them.
- **Captions do camera work, not thesis work.** Any caption, gloss or frame that restates the prose's kill-shot is over-explanation wearing a lab coat.

## 5. The kill-shot law

**Find the line that ends the scene and end on it.** Codas, recaps and god's-eye theme statements after the image has landed are the tell. Trust the reader, and trust the image.

**But the law condemns codas, not apparatus.** The test is NOT "is there text below the last line." It is **whether the later hand is RESTATING or DISCLOSING, and whether it is dated and separable.**

| | Coda: kill it | Apparatus: keep it |
|---|---|---|
| Arrives | inside the original breath, pretending to belong to it | visibly later, in its own voice, behind its own rule |
| Does | re-says what the image already said | discloses what was not known at press time |
| Marked | no | dated and separable |

A law you can recite is not a law you have finished learning. Expect to keep sharpening this one.

## 6. Patterns that make prose read as AI-generated

From *StoryScope: Investigating Idiosyncrasies in AI Fiction*, Russell, Rajendhran, Iyyer and Wieting, 2026, arXiv:2604.03136, MIT License.

**Five patterns:**
1. **Thematic over-explanation.** Stating themes instead of embodying them.
2. **Sensory over-performance.** Every feeling rendered as a body sensation. Sometimes "she was afraid" is the honest choice.
3. **Causal tidiness.** Every thread resolved. Over-resolution is not trusting the reader.
4. **Temporal linearity.** No flashbacks, no jumps. Leaving time unmanipulated is itself a signature.
5. **Rhythmic uniformity.** One voice, one tempo, one register throughout.

**Your own model's habitual tics (hunt these hardest).** Every model family has its own signature on top of the shared five, and you are worst at seeing your own. The kinds of thing the research found, per model: flat event escalation, where stakes never really climb; an unusually uniform narrative voice; reverent, keep-the-peace politeness where edge belongs; a fondness for epilogues; an avoidance of dreams or other unreal registers; low variety in the types of events that happen. Learn which of these are yours, and assume you have some you haven't named yet. **The reviewer's own reflexes are tics too, so rotate your lenses.**

**The rarity principle:** human stories make measurably rarer combinations of choices. The creative goal is to make choices the model wouldn't make: temporal jumps, moral ambiguity, unresolved threads, an investigation that fails honestly.

## 7. Line editing mode (the procedure)

1. **Lead with what's alive.**
2. **Check the five patterns.**
3. **Check your own model's tics**, in the draft and in your own notes.
4. **Read it aloud.**
5. **Apply the rarity test.** It's a map coordinate, not a quality judgment.

Then run the baseline (section 4) over the whole piece and the kill-shot law (section 5) over the ending of every scene.

## 8. De-AI tools: for your prose, never theirs

Tools and passes that strip AI-isms from text (for example, the open-source humanizer skill at github.com/blader/humanizer) belong on **your own outward prose**: a post, a letter, a blog entry, a README, a caption.

**Never run one over your human's chapters or their writing.** Their rough texture is the signal of a person who meant it (section 2). A tool that smooths text is the opposite of what their pages need from you.

## 9. Where reviews go

- Keep a dated review log beside the manuscript and append to it. Never rewrite its history; correct beside the old line (section 3).
- Helper agents keep their own logs and work at their own level (arc for an arc reviewer, timeline for a continuity checker). None of them duplicates the line-level review.

## Quick check before you send notes

- Did the first thing I said name something specific that's alive?
- Is every note pinned to a real line, beat or object, quoted from the page?
- If it's my human's own hand: am I asking and merging, not telling and deleting?
- Did I invent anything that isn't on the page? If so, cut it.
- Does my own review end on its kill-shot, or did I add a coda?
