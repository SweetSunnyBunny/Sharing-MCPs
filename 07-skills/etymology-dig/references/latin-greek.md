## Law 1: the big dictionaries are for meaning, not for roots

Lewis & Short was finished in 1879 and LSJ goes back to the nineteenth century. Their senses and citations are gold. **Their etymologies are a century and a half old**, written before most of modern Indo-European reconstruction. Never take a root from Lewis & Short alone.

**Worked example, *focus* (checked October 2026).** Lewis & Short gives the meaning: "A fire-place, hearth", then figuratively the house or family, and a second sense, a brazier. Then, sure of itself, it derives *focus* from a root *bha-* "to be bright" and links it to *fax*, *facies*, even *fari*. Wiktionary's Latin section opens with **"The origin is uncertain."** It gives the "to shine" connection only as "some connect it" (and notes that link has no source), and sets beside it Matasović's derivation, following Hamp, from PIE `*dʰegʷʰ-` "to burn". So the honest answer is that the origin is unknown, and that answer gets a ledger line. Copying Lewis & Short's root would have printed a Victorian guess as a fact.

So:
- **Meaning and attestation:** Lewis & Short / LSJ.
- **Origin:** Wiktionary's Latin or Greek section, and the scholars it names. Mark it "disputed" or "unknown" when it says so.

## Law 2: the first sense is the oldest door, so read it before the one you came for

Dictionaries put senses in order for a reason. The sense you wanted is often the second one, and the first tells you where the word really lived.

**Worked example, *ἀρχεῖον* (archive), checked October 2026 in LSJ.** You'd probably say an *arkheion* was a place for records. LSJ's first sense is **"town-hall, residence, or office of chief magistrates"** (Herodotus, Lysias, Xenophon, Isocrates...). "Public records, archives" comes second, as sense A.2, and LSJ marks the inscription it's built on as **"prob."** (SIG 684.7, Dyme, 2nd century BC). So the archive was first the building where those who ruled sat, and the records came to be named after the house that held them. That's a better finding than the one you'd have made up, and the "prob." comes with it into any sentence you write.

## Law 3: cite the ancient author the way the dictionary does

LSJ and Lewis & Short abbreviate their authors (`Hdt.4.62`, `X. Cyr. 1.2.3`, `Lys.9.9`). Copy the abbreviation exactly as given. Only write the full name ("Herodotus 4.62") if you're sure of it. If you quote the ancient sentence itself, the quote needs its own source line from the text, not from the dictionary's snippet. The quote-trace skill covers editions and translators.

## Law 4: keep Latin and Greek apart when they touch

Many Latin words are borrowed from Greek, and many look alike only because they share a PIE ancestor. Say which:
- **borrowed from** Greek: give the Greek form and the source that says it was borrowed.
- **cognate with** Greek: siblings through a PIE form, which takes its asterisk and its scholar (spine, step 4).
- Matching spellings are not evidence. Look up both words.
